    How it was made, what to keep in minddata, method, the sources of the walks and the calculator

    Roads. The Itiner-e database shows the situation around AD 150: 14,769 road segments, roughly 300,000 km. Most segments have only a conjectured course (12,952), 777 are hypothetical and only 1,040 are certain; according to the authors the location of barely 3% of the road length is known precisely. In the full Empire view the lines are simplified to 1 km precision; when zoomed in, the original full-precision lines load. The database records how the roads changed over time for only a few segments: 477 segments have a start year.

    Structure of the road network. Pažout, Brughmans, de Soto and colleagues (2026) analysed the same Itiner-e dataset. Their results in brief: Roman roads are very straight (over 1 km stretches the road is on average only 4.8% longer than the straight line), though modern main roads are straighter still; about 90% of the network is gentler than 16%, so passable for vehicles; Alpine roads are surprisingly gentle compared with those of the Taurus, the Pyrenees or the Cantabrian Mountains, which points to deliberate engineering; roads mostly run on plains and in valleys. Roman road density is high where the landscape had long been urban (Italy, Greece, Asia Minor, Tunisia) and in the military frontier zones (lower Rhine, middle and lower Danube). The location of modern main roads moderately follows that of the Roman roads. In the network the Mediterranean coastal roads are the most important intermediaries; 24 of the 45 provincial capitals rank in the top 20% of the empire-wide network for their intermediary role. The authors stress that the dataset is not equally complete everywhere (it is thinner in central Europe, the western Balkans, Tuscany and central Anatolia).

    Key routes. The layer follows the method of the article but is my own calculation: on the road network (Itiner-e; the Bosporus and Dardanelles ferries are road segments in the dataset) I found the fastest route between every pair of nodes by walking time (Tobler’s slope function, average of the two directions, 5 km/h on level road) and counted how many of these routes pass through each segment (edge betweenness). The value is the share of all pairs of the main, mainland network; the roads of Britain, Sicily, Sardinia, Crete, Cyprus and Euboea form separate networks (they have no land link in the dataset), so in the empire-wide ranking none of their segments reaches the top 20%. For these I calculated the same separately within each network (relative to their own pairs of nodes, the top 2, 5, 10 and 20% of their own road length); on the map they are shown in orange (the same four levels), and their values cannot be compared with the empire-wide ones. The road network of Corsica is too small for this (3 segments). The colours mark the top 2, 5, 10 and 20% of the network by length. Sea and river routes are not included, so the role of land roads may look larger than it was where traffic went by ship.

    Britain: Roman coin finds. Richard Henry’s corpus, published in 2024, brings together the Roman coin finds of England and Wales (from Reece 1991, Walton 2012, the Roman Rural Settlement project, the Portable Antiquities Scheme up to 2022 and other sources). The layer uses the parish table recommended by the author: 487,994 coins from 5,692 parishes, by the 21 Reece issue periods (1 = to AD 41 … 21 = 388–402). Columns 22–24 of the table hold 68 coins in total; their periods are not given in the documentation, so they are left out. Each circle stands at the centre of the parish, not at the find spot, and its area is proportional to the number of coins of the selected period. The numbers also reflect research intensity: metal-detector finds recorded by the PAS and large excavations yield many coins even where Roman coin use was not exceptional. Differences between periods also depend on how much was struck (far more small bronzes of the 3rd–4th centuries circulated), so a parish’s coin profile is best compared with the British mean, as the author does.

    Danubian sanctuaries. In the Danubian provinces (Raetia, Noricum, Pannonia, Moesia Superior and Inferior, Dacia) the Temples layer has been extended with the 325 sanctuaries of Csaba Szabó’s Digital Atlas of Roman Sanctuaries in the Danubian Provinces (DAS), with the author’s consent of 24 November 2026. According to the atlas’s legend, 224 are archaeologically attested, 21 epigraphically attested and 80 presumed. The atlas’s own settlement points (within 5 km, in the same province) are shown on the card as the settlement; the descriptive texts are not taken over. 36 sanctuaries lie within 300 m of a temple point from Muccigrosso; for these the card notes that the same sanctuary may appear in the other database. According to the author, some sanctuaries discovered after 2020 are not yet included.

    Roman stone monuments of south-eastern Slovenia. Edisa Lozić’s (ZRC SAZU) dataset of 194 Roman stone monuments (funerary stelae, altars, milestones, inscribed plaques, architectural elements) along the Emona–Siscia road in Dolenjska and Bela Krajina, with WGS84 coordinates (Zenodo, CC BY 4.0; Journal of Open Archaeology Data 2026). One monument is left out because its coordinates are wrong (a latitude value stands in place of the longitude). Monuments with identical coordinates are merged into one point (119 points); a lighter point marks one monument, a darker one several. The card shows the type, where the piece is kept, the stone, the size, the date, the inscription and the source’s references, and where available the Ubi erat lupa ID as a link.

    Trismegistos and EDH links. On the settlement cards the Trismegistos Places link comes from DARE; where DARE has none, it follows the Trismegistos ID in Wikidata (P1958), but only if there is a single ID and the geographic database of the Epigraphic Database Heidelberg gives no conflicting ID (about 850 places). The EDH findspot row lists the findspots with inscriptions in the EDH geographic database that EDH explicitly assigns to this ancient place by its Pleiades ID (not merely as a place near it) and that lie within 10 km of the map point. If there are at most three, all are listed; if more, the general (unspecified) findspot of the town. Inscription counts follow the EDH open data (CC BY-SA 4.0). Lupa.at (Ubi erat lupa) offers no page per findspot, so it is not linked.

    Excavations (Fasti Online). Fasti Online is the database of the International Association of Classical Archaeology (AIAC) on excavations in the classical world, mainly since 2000; it is most complete for Italy, Bulgaria and Romania. The layer uses its site records and excavation seasons (retrieved on 24 November 2026 from the public data interface). Only sites with coordinates and at least one phase falling between 100 BC and AD 600 are shown; where the area came under Roman rule before 100 BC (Italy, Sicily, Sardinia, Hispania, Macedonia, Achaea, Africa, Asia, Narbonensis), counting from the conquest (using the years of the “when did it become Roman?” view), so that Roman phases of the Republican period are shown too (99 sites, mainly in southern and central Italy). In total 6,756 sites, 4,097 of them with recorded excavation seasons (7,996 in total; the seasons in Greece and Cyprus come from Archaeology in Greece Online / Chronique des fouilles, as taken over by Fasti Online). The date “0000/0000” is treated as unknown. Location, the classification of the excavated remains and dating are those of Fasti Online; rarer remain types keep their English names. The “Excavations nearby” row on the settlement cards lists sites within 5 km, nearest first.

    Rhine area: countermarked coins. A countermark is a small stamp (a legend or a symbol) punched into a coin already in circulation. Ulrich Werz’s database collects Early Imperial countermarked bronze coins found in the Rhine area and beyond, based on a revised catalogue of his 2009 book (Gegenstempel auf frühkaiserzeitlichen Aesprägungen im Rheingebiet, Winterthur). Of its 13,132 records, 9,460 coins have a find spot with coordinates; they appear at 372 points (merged by Werz’s find-spot number). The period selector follows Werz’s dating; where the dating wavers between two periods (e.g. “mid-Augustan or Claudian”), the coin is placed in the “uncertain” group. Each circle’s area is proportional to the number of coins of the selected period. High numbers mainly mark well-researched military sites (e.g. Vindonissa, Nijmegen, Neuss).

    Provinces. The DARE provincial boundaries follow the Trajanic situation (c. AD 117), with the Augustan regions (I–XI) in Italy. The “extent” buttons show the outlines of the Ancient World Mapping Center around 60 BC, AD 117 and AD 200. This line is off by default, because in several places (for example in Dacia) it differs markedly from the DARE provincial boundaries. On two stretches the DARE boundary cut off the forts of the limes, and elsewhere it extended beyond it; there I aligned the pale province shading with the limes line: Britannia up to Hadrian’s Wall (the line ran about 25 km further south, leaving even Luguvalium/Carlisle outside), Germania Superior and Raetia up to the Upper German–Raetian limes (the Wetterau forts lay 3–13 km outside, while behind the Main and the outer limes the shading reached beyond the frontier). Forts just behind the limes stay inside with a 0.5 km tolerance for the digitisation of the line; new coastal parts follow the AWMC coastline of c. AD 200. Here the DARE shading already follows the outer limes of the mid-2nd century, not the Odenwald–Neckar line of AD 117; Hadrian’s Wall was also built after AD 122, but runs only a few km north of the Trajanic Stanegate line. Not aligned: the Antonine Wall and the Limes Transalutanus (later frontiers) and the Olt line (at most 2 km difference). River frontiers follow the main channel of the river (OpenStreetMap): on the Danube Raetia from Eining, Noricum, Pannonia Superior and Inferior, Moesia Superior and Inferior to the head of the delta (from the Banat to the mouth of the Olt the left bank is Dacia), on the Rhine Germania Inferior. In the Netherlands it follows the Roman branch of the Rhine (Nederrijn – Kromme Rijn – Leidse Rijn – Oude Rijn to Katwijk); as the modern channel there deviates by up to 1 km from the ancient one, the forts along it stay inside with a 1.2 km tolerance. Left-bank bridgehead forts (e.g. Contra Aquincum, Kelamantia, Divitia/Deutz) lie outside the province, as they stood across the river in reality. The map’s Danube line from its source to the head of the delta is also the OpenStreetMap main channel. The coastlines of the shading follow the AWMC sea of c. AD 200 (the sea of the map): parts reaching into the sea were cut off with a 0.8 km tolerance, and the coastal strip of land left outside (within 4 km of the sea and 5 km of the shading) as well as the missing islands (e.g. Delos, Malta, the Dalmatian and Aegean islands; at most 25 km from the shading) were assigned to the nearest province. No extension north of Hadrian’s Wall or north of the Rhine mouth; the Isle of Man was not included. After adding the coastal strip, land holes left inside the shading and pieces of land enclosed only by a province and water (e.g. lagoon sandbars, islands in river mouths) were assigned to the neighbouring province. River lines end at the same coastline (several modern mouths lie further out). The Tiber line is the modern channel from OpenStreetMap; at Ostia the ancient channel ran closer to the town in the bend cut off in 1557, for which no accurate, freely usable line exists. Where the channel has changed much since antiquity, the ancient course is shown with the AWMC river lines (after the Barrington Atlas): the branches of the Nile delta (Kanobikos, Bolbitinos, Sebennytikos, Thermoutiakos, Bousiritikos, Mendesios, Tanitikos, Pelousiakos, Agathos Daimon), the Po delta, the Maeander (to the then shore of the Latmian Gulf), and the Euphrates and Tigris in Mesopotamia. The main channel of the Rhine from its source to the Pannerdensche Kop and the Waal (Vahalis) are the modern OpenStreetMap channels (the Upper Rhine was straightened in the 19th century, so the Roman channel there meandered more); in the Netherlands the Roman main branch is shown (Nederrijn – Kromme Rijn – Leidse Rijn – Oude Rijn, to Katwijk). Modern canals (Suez and Ismailia canals) and the later Lek branch were removed (v331). The Natural Earth lakes included modern reservoirs; these were removed because they did not exist in antiquity: Lake Nasser (in Nubia the AWMC pre-dam channel of the Nile is now shown), Lake Assad, the Atatürk and Karakaya dam lakes, the Keban reservoir, the Spanish reservoirs (Buendía, Entrepeñas, Cíjara, Orellana, García de Sola, Alcántara/Valdecañas) and the 20th-century IJsselmeer (the ancient Lake Flevo and the inlets are in the AWMC water layer) (v333). The AWMC coastline is accurate to about 1 km, so some coastal sites and road ends fell on the water (v332). Where a site’s position is well known (accurate to 200 m or better according to DARE) and it lies no more than 1 km from the shore, a narrow 0.5 km wide spit of land was cut out of the water from the point to the shore (87 sites); likewise where roads meet in the water close to the shore (25 junctions). Real island sites that AWMC does not draw (Arados, Ilha do Pessegueiro, Torcello, Isola Santo Ianni) appear as small islands. Sites more than 1 km out (Castra Corneliana?, Ostrakine, Korbilon, Wierde, Conobaria) were left as they are, because there the ancient shore really ran elsewhere or the location is uncertain. Settlements whose location is known only to about 2 km (e.g. Iscalis?) may stay on the water; the ends of roads finishing in the water (26 roads, about 12 km in total) were cut back to the shore. Stretches of a few hundred metres of coastal roads that fall on the water and genuine crossings (e.g. the causeway to Meninx, modern Djerba) were kept. The shading of Dacia and Arabia was extended to the Roman forts and settlements that fell outside because of DARE’s generalised boundary (Dacia: Resculum, Rădăcineşti, Castra Traiana, Slăveni, Bulci, Angustia, Comidava; Arabia: sites in the Hauran and along the Via Nova Traiana, e.g. Tricomia, Sounoua, Umm el-Quttein, er-Ruweihi, and Modiana and Laba near Hegra). Forts of later frontier lines stay outside (Limes Transalutanus; in Arabia forts built after 193, e.g. Jurf ed-Darawish, el-Mutrab, Castra Praetorii Mobeni), as does the western Banat (Vršac, Dupljaja). Likewise added: in Syria Veriaraca and Cunna (stations of the Palmyra–Damascus road) and the island of Arados, in Egypt the praesidia of the Eastern Desert (Compasi, Barramiya, Iteima, Simiou, Hamra) and Tamiathis, in Mauretania Tingitana the forts around Sala and Volubilis, in Moesia Inferior Argamum. The Severan forts of the Limes Tripolitanus and the left-bank Danube bridgeheads stay outside.

    Settlements and bases. By the DARE types (based on the Barrington Atlas): “major settlement” (city), “civitas capital”, “settlement”, “major fort”, “fort”, “fortlet”, “camp”. The “major fort” category is mostly legionary fortresses, but it is not a complete list of legions: some legionary bases (e.g. Eboracum) appear as cities, and it also contains a few large late antique forts. DARE date limits are the period limits of the Barrington Atlas, not precise dates. Sites without a known ancient name (3,542 of the smaller settlements, 1,247 of the forts) appear only as dots without a label; labels are placed without overlap, so in dense areas not every name is shown. Every dot can be clicked.

    Place records. Clicking a place combines data from five sources: the DARE place record (late Roman province, area, location precision, sites belonging to it, links), Pleiades (description, feature type, periods, name variants; matched through the Pleiades link of DARE; the description is in English), Wikidata (Wikipedia articles and Commons image: primarily from the Wikidata item given by DARE, otherwise via the Pleiades ID), the LIST inscription dataset and the CHRE coin hoard database. Inscriptions are counted in two ways: by the ancient name of the findspot (EDCS, EDH) or its Pleiades ID within 15 km, and separately by findspots within 2 km. EDCS often places the findspot at the centre of the modern town, so for large cities the 2 km figure is low (Aquincum: 1, by name 2,392); the figure by name, on the other hand, can include inscriptions from the city’s territory. The type is the cleaned LIST classification, or the machine classification where there is none, which can be wrong; “identification inscription, stamp” includes brick and other stamps. For coin hoards the hoards found within 10 km of the place are counted. The image is the main image (P18) of the place’s Wikidata item, from Wikimedia Commons: it often shows the modern town or the ruins, not necessarily the ancient state. Author and licence are given under each image from the Commons data; images were scaled down to 280 pixels wide, otherwise unchanged. Only images with a known free licence (CC, public domain) are shown; otherwise only the Commons link remains.

    Society, economy. Person data (gender, social status, age, origin) exist only for inscriptions taken from EDH, about 9% of the LIST inscriptions (89,192 people on 44,924 inscriptions); the figures therefore come from only part of a place’s inscriptions. There are hardly any person data from Italy, because Italian inscriptions are processed by EDR, not EDH. Where the 2 km radius yields more inscriptions than matching by name, people and centuries are counted from the 2 km radius (this is marked). Social status refers to everyone named in the inscription, not only local residents: the senatorial order may include, for example, governors and consuls mentioned in the text. Age comes mostly from epitaphs, so it is age at death; Roman inscriptions often rounded ages (30, 40, 50 years), and children and women are under-represented, so this is not the mortality of the population. Origin is given as the inscription states it (in Latin form). Inscriptions are assigned to a century by the midpoint of their dating, using only those with a dating range of at most 200 years. ORBIS figures are model results (fastest route, and cheapest route by donkey, river and sea vessels), not recorded journeys; they are shown only where the ORBIS site has the same name as the map place within 25 km, or lies within 3 km (this is marked). For mints, the number of coin types is the number of types in the catalogues linked to nomisma.org, not the quantity of coins struck; the large Roman catalogues (RIC) are better covered than Greek civic coinage. Ports come from A. de Graauw’s catalogue, matched by DARE or Pleiades ID, otherwise by name (within 10 km); by the author’s own account the list of structures is not complete.

    Italy. Italian places have three further sources: (1) the open EpiDoc release of the Epigraphic Database Roma (EDR), assigned to places by the EDR ancient place-name field (99,019 inscriptions at 417 places, Rome included). The open EDR release has no structured person data, so markers (father’s name, freedman, slave, imperial freedman and slave, military terms, Augustalis) and ages are searched in the inscription texts with the same method as on the Rome map; the figures count inscriptions, not people, and text search can err. (2) Records of the Geoportale Nazionale per l’Archeologia (GNA) dated Roman or late antique (60,444 of 146,142 sites or finds) within 3 km of cities and 1.5 km elsewhere, grouped by type. GNA mainly collects data from preventive and licensed excavations, field surveys and older inventories, with uneven density across the country: long-known major sites (e.g. the interior of Ostia or Paestum) often hardly appear, so the number does not measure a place’s importance. (3) From the Ministry of Culture’s “Luoghi della cultura” database: archaeological areas, archaeological parks, funerary monuments and (by their name) archaeological museums open to visitors within 3 km, 4 km for cities.

    Hispania and amphorae. In Castile and León, Roman-period sites (classified as Republican, early Imperial or late Roman; 4,777 of about 23,000) from the archaeological site catalogue of the Junta de Castilla y León within 3 km of cities and 1.5 km elsewhere, taking the extent of the site into account; names link to the catalogue’s official record. Inscribed amphorae from the CEIPAC database (Roman Open Data) are aggregated by find place: each locality goes to the nearest city, civitas capital, base or fort within 8 km (otherwise to any place within 3 km), so each amphora counts only once. Type names carry the usual origin and content of the type in brackets; this is typical of the type, not measured on each piece. The map shows my own aggregates. The wine and oil production sites (Y. Peña Cervantes’s catalogue, 553 sites) have no coordinates, only modern country, province and municipality: each site was placed at the Wikidata coordinate of its municipality (in Portugal its freguesia), which worked for 503; each site goes to the nearest map place within 15 km (421 sites at 161 places). The location is therefore only as precise as the municipality; “product” follows the catalogue (with a question mark = uncertain).

    Slovenia. From the cultural heritage register of the Slovenian Ministry of Culture (Register nepremične kulturne dediščine, RNPD), the Roman-dated items (2,038 of 31,107; dated “early Roman”, “Roman period” or “late Roman”, mostly archaeological sites, a few buildings) within 3 km of cities and 1.5 km elsewhere; the names link to the register’s record. The type is the item’s first classifiable keyword. The map has few places on Slovenian territory (mainly the cities, a few settlements and forts), so only 94 items appear, at 17 places; the other sites are not near any place on the map.

    North Macedonia. I found no open state site database; the archaeological sites in Wikidata (CC0) whose stated period is classical antiquity, late antiquity, early Christianity or explicitly ancient Rome or the Roman Empire (2,388 sites; queried on 13 November 2026), within 3 km of cities and 1.5 km elsewhere (289 sites at 58 places). The periods in Wikidata carry no source reference and probably come from the Macedonian Wikipedia site articles; “classical antiquity” may include the Hellenistic period, so these are “ancient or late antique”, not necessarily Roman, sites. The name is the English label where there is one, otherwise a Latin transliteration of the Macedonian name with the Cyrillic name in brackets; the link goes to the Macedonian Wikipedia article (or, if there is none, to the Wikidata item).

    Romania. From the PDFs of 31 counties (Dacia, Moesia Inferior and the Danube) of the Romanian list of historic monuments (Lista monumentelor istorice 2015, Monitorul Oficial 113 bis/2016; an official administrative text) I extracted all 20,940 entries (two entries were missed), and use the archaeological entries dated Roman (Epoca romană, daco-romană, romano-bizantină etc.) and the Dacian entries dated AD (Latène, Geto-Dacian): 1,272 entries. The list has no coordinates: the location comes from the entry’s Wikidata item via the LMI code (discarded if more than 15 km from the stated settlement), failing that from its parent site, otherwise from the Wikidata coordinate of the village or commune. Entries with an exact location count within 3 km of cities and 1.5 km elsewhere; entries located only by settlement go to the nearest map place within 5 km (marked ≈). 526 entries appear at 200 places. “Roman” follows the dating in the list; the late Roman or Romano-Byzantine category holds entries dated to the 4th–6th centuries or Byzantine.

    France. The national archaeological map (Patriarche) is not public, so I use three open sources, each within 3 km of cities and 1.5 km elsewhere: (1) from the Ministry of Culture’s Mérimée list, the buildings and sites protected as historic monuments whose main construction period is Gallo-Roman, ancient or 1st–5th century AD (738 entries with coordinates, at 188 places); the names link to the official POP record.     Caesar’s campaigns (walks). Under “Caesar’s campaigns” above the map, one walk per year follows Caesar’s campaigns, primarily after his own accounts, the Gallic War (Bellum Gallicum) and the Civil War (Bellum civile), and the books by other authors attached to them (Hirtius’ eighth book of the Gallic War; the Alexandrian, African and Spanish Wars). Numbers in brackets give book and chapter. Julian dates come from the chronology of John T. Ramsey and Kurt A. Raaflaub; the Roman calendar then differed from the true season. Places are the map’s DARE-based points or locations from the Pleiades gazetteer; where the site of a battle or crossing is uncertain, the text says what the marker is based on. The line between stops is a route found on the legion calculator’s network (Itiner-e, at sea ORBIS): in Caesar’s time there were no Roman roads in Gaul yet, so it shows only the likely direction given by the terrain, not Caesar’s known route. Where the source gives the time of a march, a button lets the calculator compute the same march for comparison; the calculator uses full-strength legions, while Caesar’s legions were probably smaller.

    Ancient journeys (walks). The “Ancient journeys” and “Campaigns” cards offer walks based on contemporary or near-contemporary accounts. The journey of the Bordeaux pilgrim (AD 333) is based on the Itinerarium Burdigalense: the stops are the text’s larger towns and comments, the mileages are the text’s section totals (after Andrew Jacobs’ English translation). At the end of a section the walk shows how many km the text’s mileage corresponds to (1 Roman mile ≈ 1.48 km) and how long the route found on the map’s road network is; the two may differ, because the pilgrim’s road was not always the shortest, and the numbers differ in places between manuscripts, nor do the listed distances between stations always add up to the text’s totals. Julian’s journey down the Danube (AD 361) follows books 21–22 of Ammianus Marcellinus; of the dates Ammianus gives only that of the entry into Constantinople, nor does he name the place where Julian took to the boats on the Danube, so the marker there shows only the upper Danube. Numbers in brackets give book, chapter and section.

    The journeys of the Apostle Paul (walks). Four walks follow the account of the Acts of the Apostles: the three missionary journeys and the voyage to Rome with the shipwreck. The book gives many ports, routes and lengths of stay, but no years; the only external anchor of the chronology is Gallio’s proconsulship of Achaea (AD 51–52 according to an inscription at Delphi), so the years are only approximate. On land the line is the route found on the map’s road network; at sea, where the text moves from port to port, the line consists of straight stretches through the named ports, islands and capes, because the map’s network of sea routes is too sparse for this. For two sea stretches (Philippi–Troas, Rhegium–Puteoli) the calculator can also compute the voyage; the calculator treats the seasons of Roman sailing after Vegetius (4.39): safe between 27 May and 14 September, uncertain until 11 November, closed between 11 November and 10 March. The dates for these calculations are estimates.

    More ancient journeys. Hannibal’s march (218 BC) follows book III of Polybius (with Livy as a supplement); Polybius gives distances in stades, and we have reckoned the km values with a stade of about 185 m. The site of the Alpine pass is unknown; the marker shows one of the proposed passes (Mont Genèvre), and there the line follows the later Roman road only for orientation. The sites of the Rhone crossing, the “Island”, the Ticinus and the Trebia are also known only approximately, and the text says so in each case. Horace’s journey to Brundisium (38 or 37 BC) follows Satires 1.5; the little town “whose name won’t fit the verse” is taken by the ancient commentator to be Equus Tuticus, which is uncertain. Rutilius Namatianus’ voyage home (AD 416 or 417) follows the first book of De reditu suo and the fragment of the second; the division into days comes from the introduction of the Loeb edition, and at sea the line consists of straight stretches. Short quotations from the poems are our own translations. Germanicus’ journey in the East (AD 18–19) follows Tacitus, Annals 2.53–61 and 2.69–73; Tacitus gives the order but hardly any days, so the sea lines show only the direction. Cicero’s journey to Cilicia (51–50 BC) follows Cicero’s letters to Atticus and to his friends; the dates follow the Roman calendar before Julius Caesar’s reform. Pliny the Younger’s journey to Bithynia (AD 109/111) follows letters 15–18 of book X of the Letters; the year is disputed, the day of arrival (17 September) is certain. Arrian’s tour of the Black Sea (AD 131/132) follows the first part of the Periplus; distances given in stades were converted with a stade of about 185 m; the § numbers are those of W. Falconer’s translation. Egeria’s pilgrimage (AD 383–384) follows the surviving part of the Itinerarium Egeriae; the years rest on P. Devos’s reckoning; the lines of the excursions from Jerusalem start from Jerusalem. Hadrian’s journeys (AD 117–134) follow the emperor’s return journey of 117–118, his two great journeys and the African inspection in seven walks. The days in Asia Minor in 117 come from an itinerary inscription found in Rome that names neither emperor nor year (CIL VI 5076); linking it to Hadrian is the conjecture of W. Weber and A. von Domaszewski, whereas the letter from Juliopolis (IGR IV 349) is a certain date. The ancient framework comes from the Historia Augusta life of Hadrian (chapters 10–14, 20–26) and book 69 of Cassius Dio; the more exact chronology and the cities visited were pieced together by scholars from inscriptions, coins and papyri (H. Halfmann 1986, A. R. Birley 2003) – the text gives these as “according to Birley” and marks the question-marked places as uncertain. Where the source names no place (e.g. the Parthian talks or the Libyan lion hunt), the marker is for orientation only. Of the Lambaesis speech inscription and Julia Balbilla’s poems only our own summaries are given. Trajan’s campaigns (AD 101–117) follow the two Dacian wars and the Parthian war in four walks. The main source is the Byzantine epitome of book 68 of Cassius Dio; of Trajan’s own Dacian commentaries a single sentence survives (in Priscian). The departure days come from inscribed records (Acta fratrum Arvalium: 25 March 101; Fasti Ostienses: 4 June 105), and the death of Decebalus is also recorded on the tombstone of Ti. Claudius Maximus. Linking the scenes of Trajan’s Column to places is scholarly interpretation; the years and order of the Parthian war (including Malalas’ dates) are disputed, and the walk follows the summary of the Encyclopaedia Iranica. Aurelian’s campaigns (AD 270–275) follow the Italian and Danubian fighting, the Palmyrene war and the Gallic war in three walks. The main source is book 1 of Zosimus (public-domain English translation of 1814), alongside Eutropius, the Epitome de Caesaribus and Aurelius Victor. The Historia Augusta life of Aurelian is used only where scholars consider it reliable (e.g. the defeat at Placentia), otherwise with a label; the fights at Immae and Daphne are separated following G. Downey. The sources contradict each other on Zenobia’s fate, and the walk says so. The journeys of St Martin (c. 336–397) follow Sulpicius Severus’ Life of Martin (Vita Martini) and his Letter 3 to Bassula (English translation by A. Roberts, 1894, public domain); the place and year of death come from Gregory of Tours. The Vita is hagiography: it tells of miracles, gives no dates and narrates the events of the episcopate out of order; the years are scholarly estimates, and the year of birth (316 or 336) is disputed. Priscus’ embassy to Attila (AD 449) follows Priscus’ fragment 8 (English translation by J. B. Bury, public domain), and the return journey and the exposure of the plot after Gibbon’s summary (Decline and Fall, chapter 34). North of the Danube Priscus names no place that can be identified today: the crossing, Attila’s tents and his residence are only symbolic points, and the identification of the rivers (Drecon, Tigas, Tiphesas) is disputed. Julian’s Persian campaign (363) follows the eyewitness account of Ammianus Marcellinus (books 23–25); some places inside Persia cannot be identified, and there the line is straight. Sidonius’ journey to Rome (467) follows Sidonius Apollinaris’ letter 1.5. St Severinus in Noricum (c. 454–488) follows Eugippius’ Life (511); the work is arranged by places, so the walk shows places, not a single journey. Paula’s pilgrimage (385–386) follows Jerome’s Letter 108; the sea lines are approximate. The Jewish War (66–74) follows Josephus, with dates as converted by J. Lendering (Livius.org); Josephus’ figures are often exaggerated. Agricola in Britain (77–84) follows Tacitus’ Agricola; Tacitus numbers the summers and in Scotland names only estuaries and one mountain, so the points there are symbolic. Ignatius’ journey to Rome (c. 110) follows Ignatius’ letters and Polycarp’s letter; only six places are certain, the rest is conjecture. Constantine’s campaign of 312 follows the panegyric of 313, Lactantius, Eusebius and Zosimus; the two accounts of the vision contradict each other. The Pannonian–Dalmatian revolt (6–9) follows the eyewitness account of Velleius Paterculus and books 55–56 of Cassius Dio; the sites of the Volcaean marshes, the river Bathinus and Arduba are uncertain. Valentinian I in Pannonia (374–375) and Adrianople (376–378) follow books 29–31 of Ammianus Marcellinus; the exact sites of the battle of Adrianople and of the Danube crossing are not known. The journey of St Augustine (354–391) follows the Confessions and Possidius’ Life; the years are scholars’ calculations. Scipio Africanus (210–202) follows Livy books 26–30, compared with Polybius; the battlefields (Baecula, Ilipa, Zama) are known only approximately. Marius against the Cimbri (105–101), the revolt of Spartacus (73–71), Crassus and Carrhae (55–53) and Antony’s Parthian campaign (36) follow mainly Plutarch’s Lives (Marius, Crassus, Antony), for Spartacus together with Appian; the figures are mostly exaggerated, and several sites (Marius’ Rhône camp, Spartacus’ last battle, the battlefield of Carrhae, Phraata) are uncertain. The conquest of Britain and Boudica (43–61) follows Cassius Dio books 60 and 62 and Tacitus’ Annals (14.29–39); the sites of the landing, the river battle and Boudica’s last battle are scholarly conjectures. Corbulo in Armenia (54–66) follows Tacitus’ Annals (13–15) and Dio; the sites of Rhandeia and Volandum are uncertain. The year of the four emperors (69) follows Tacitus’ Histories books 1–3, with dates after W. H. Fyfe’s summary; the sites of the Bedriacum battlefields are disputed.

    Varus, Marcus Aurelius, the Amber Road and Ovid. Varus and Germanicus (AD 9–16) follows the accounts of Cassius Dio, Velleius Paterculus and Tacitus; Kalkriese (excavated since 1987) is today generally held to be one site of the battle, but this is disputed, and most places beyond the Rhine (Varus’ summer camp, Idistaviso, the Angrivarian wall) are unknown; there the markers show only the wider area. For the Marcomannic wars of Marcus Aurelius (166–180) there is no continuous source: Dio survives only in a Byzantine abridgement, and the emperor’s route is unknown in most places. The stops are places certainly or probably connected with him (the notes between two books of the Meditations: “among the Quadi, at the Granua” and “at Carnuntum”; the rock inscription of Laugaricio at Trenčín; the archaeological site of Mušov); a stay at Aquincum cannot be supported from the sources, and the text says so. The Amber Road is a thematic walk with Pliny’s account (37.45) and the archaeological sites of the road, which also crosses today’s Hungary (Salla, Savaria, Scarbantia); north of Carnuntum the course is unknown. Ovid’s exile (AD 8–9) is a literary walk based on the poems of the Tristia; the port of departure (Brundisium) and the site of Tempyra are uncertain, and no poem describes the stages of the overland journey through Thrace.

The sites of the battles (Issus, Lugdunum, Ctesiphon, Hatra) are known, but in most places the routes of the armies are not; the line shows only the probable direction, with straight stretches in Mesopotamia.     Legion march calculator. The panel opened with the button in the top-left corner of the map finds a route between two places and estimates a march schedule. Network: the Itiner-e road network (segment ends merged within 0.5 km; length, average slope, road type, certainty) and the ORBIS coastal, open-sea, river and ferry links; ports connect to the roads within 25 km. Marching speed after Vegetius (Epitoma rei militaris I.9: the normal military step covers 20 Roman miles and the full step 24 miles in five summer hours): 4.7 and 5.7 km/h on level ground; a forced march lasts 7 hours. The daily marching time is Vegetius’ five (in a forced march seven) “summer hours”, counted as midsummer hours at the latitude of Rome (one summer hour is about 1¼ modern hours, i.e. about 6¼ and 8¾ hours). On short days the march ends earlier: by sunset the tail of the column must also arrive and the evening marching camp (castra) must be built. Camp building takes 3 hours by default (modern estimates range from 2 to 5 hours; this can be changed in the panel, and the camp can be switched off) and starts when the head of the column arrives; the column length follows from the numbers of men, pack animals and wagons. A large army is therefore slower in winter than a small one; it slows with the relief depending on direction (Tobler’s function: uphill slower than downhill) and slightly on conjectured and hypothetical roads. Relief comes from the elevations of the Itiner-e tracks and, where these are missing (mainly Hispania, Gaul, Britain, Italy and the Alps), from the Terrain Tiles elevation grid (c. 200–300 m): the track was sampled about every 200 m, smoothed with a 1 km moving average, and slope, ascent and highest point were computed from pieces of at least 250 m. The daily stages show the ascent; from November to April crossings above 1,200 m are flagged. Because Roman road tracks are often only approximate, the elevation profile can cut across ridges; therefore, using the real elevations recorded in Wikidata for 37 main passes used in antiquity (e.g. Alpis Poenina/Great St Bernard, Alpis Graia, Alpis Cottia/Montgenèvre, Mont Cenis, Brenner, Reschen, Julier, Septimer, Somport, Col du Perthus, Cilician Gates, Zigana), the profiles of road segments within 15 km were capped at the highest nearby pass + 100 m (20 segments). Where no known pass is nearby, ascent and highest point may still be overestimated in mountains; river crossings and day-to-day weather are not modelled. Seasonal effects (can be switched off; the multipliers are estimates, not values taken from a source, and can be changed in the panel): from December to April passes above 1,500 m are closed by snow (if this forces a detour, the calculator says so); in the rainy season (north of the Alps October–April, around the Mediterranean November–March, in the south December–February) mud slows the march, by default 5% on certain, 15% on conjectured and 25% on hypothetical roads; in summer heat (in the south, mainly below 38°) the daily march is shorter (80%) and water needs are higher (1.7×); in the grazing season horses and mules get only hard feed from the baggage (4 and 2.5 kg). At sea sailing is 10% slower in the uncertain season and 30% slower in the closed season (if allowed); from May to September east of 10° E the Etesian north wind makes sailing towards the south-south-east faster and towards the north-north-west slower (at most ±30%, based on the roughly 1.5-fold direction dependence of the ORBIS July travel times Alexandria–Rome and Antioch–Rome). On rivers north of 44° N ice stops navigation from December to February, and low water slows it by 20% in August–September; on the Nile the flood speeds downstream travel by 30% from August to October and low water slows it by 20% from April to June. The route search uses, for every segment, the season of the day it is reached, counted from the departure date. Cavalry alone moves at 7 km/h, a column with ox wagons at most 3.5 km/h. Sea speed is 160 km/day by default, rivers 65 km/day downstream and 15 upstream (after the ORBIS averages); the closed winter sailing season (Vegetius IV.39: 11 November – 10 March) is avoided by default and the uncertain season is flagged. Column length and grain, fodder and water needs rest on assumptions that can be changed in the panel (e.g. 0.85 kg grain per man per day, one pack mule per eight soldiers). Dates follow the Julian calendar and are also shown as Roman dates. All of this is an estimate, not a march time recorded in a source.

    Off-road marching (November 2026). With “Off-road too”, the calculator searches a 9 km terrain grid as well as the Roman road network, and leaves the road where that is faster (e.g. in Mesopotamia, where few roads are known). The grid is built from the Terrain Tiles elevation data (see the Relief layer): each cell has a mean elevation, a terrain factor (the mean of Tobler’s slope function over the 1.5 km slopes inside the cell, expressing how broken the ground is), and a flag where a major river (Natural Earth) lies towards a neighbouring cell – crossing it costs as much time as an unbridged crossing on the roads. Sea and lakes are impassable. The speed on level open ground is 0.5 times the road speed (adjustable in the panel); this is my own assumption: modern US Army doctrine (FM 101-10, 1961) gives an average of 2.5 mph on roads and 1 mph cross-country for foot troops, but that includes terrain, which the grid counts separately. The grid knows nothing of forest, marsh, fords or ancient tracks, so off-road legs are only rough estimates; they are dashed on the map.

    Further parts of the calculator. Snapping to the road: the start, via and destination points are attached to the nearest point of the nearest road segment, and the route continues along the appropriate part of that segment. Previously they were attached to the nearest road node, which on long segments was sometimes 30–50 km away (e.g. 49 km for Matisco, Mâcon); of the towns, civitas capitals and legionary bases, 266 instead of 494 now remain more than 5 km from the road network (mostly places through which Itiner-e has no road). River crossings: where a road segment crosses one of the larger rivers shown on the map (Natural Earth), the calculator reports a crossing; if a bridge recorded in Wikidata as Roman lies within 2 km (555 bridges, e.g. Trajan’s Bridge and the bridges of Mérida, Alcántara and Mainz), it names it, otherwise it adds a delay of 2 hours by default (ferry, pontoon bridge or ford; this can be changed in the panel). Only 34 of the 762 crossings have a known bridge because the bridge list is incomplete, so the delay is rather an upper estimate. If a track crosses a river an even number of times, I take it as the zigzag of an imprecise track, not as a crossing. Mountain elevations: in mountains the imprecise track can cut across ridges. Therefore, for every segment reaching above 500 m, I searched a ±4 km band around the track for the lowest passage connecting its two ends (Terrain Tiles relief) and capped the profile 50 m above it (991 segments; the highest point fell by 74 m on average). At some known passes the real and the computed highest point are: Loibl 1,367 m / 1,377 m, Little St Bernard 2,188 m / 2,230 m, Pyhrn 945 m / 1,054 m; where the real passage lies outside the band, a larger error may remain. Age of roads: by default only roads that already existed in the departure year are used, but only where Itiner-e gives a start year (477 segments). The builder-emperor field is not used because it often reflects a later milestone or repair (e.g. Constantine the Great for the Vienna–Brigantio road). If this makes the route longer, a warning is shown. Sailing: besides the days at sea there is an estimate for favourable and unfavourable winds: according to Casson, ancient ships averaged 4–6 knots with favourable and 2–2.5 knots with unfavourable winds; relative to the set average speed (by default about 3.6 knots) I use factors of 1.4 and 0.55. Via point, camps, profile: an intermediate place (e.g. a pass or town) can be given that the route must pass. The night camps on the map can be clicked to show the matching row of the daily table. The elevation profile is drawn from the elevations of the nodes (segment ends) and the highest point of each segment, so it is a rough sketch.

    Check against recorded marches. (1) In 49 BC Caesar’s six legions marched from Corfinium to Brundisium; according to Cicero’s letters and Caesar they left on 21 February and arrived on 9 March (Roman calendar; 2–18 January in the Julian calendar), c. 460 km according to Raaflaub and Ramsey, about 16½ days with two rest days. The calculator (30,000 infantry, 720 cavalry, 3,840 mules, 2 January, 447 km) gives 22 days at the normal step and 18 days at the full step (a rest day after every 7 marching days). (2) In 57 BC eight legions reached the border of the Belgae from Vesontio “in about 15 days” (Gallic War II.2; at the Matrona, the Marne, according to Raaflaub and Ramsey); for Vesontio–Durocortorum (Reims, 287 km) in summer the calculator gives 13 days, i.e. in summer it is somewhat faster than Caesar’s average (20–23 km a day according to Raaflaub and Ramsey, and a “normal day’s march” of c. 24 km). The difference may also be explained by the lack of Roman roads in 1st-century BC Gaul, by supply and by reconnaissance. After this check I changed the calculation of marching time: it used to be counted in Roman hours, which gave unduly short marches in winter (Corfinium–Brundisium at the normal step: 42 days).
